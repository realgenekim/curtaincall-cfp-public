The thesis has the right diagnosis—code scarcity is collapsing—but it jumps too quickly from “code gets cheap” to “SaaS rents disappear” and “FDEs capture the value.”

## 1. Weakest links

**The contest is not a controlled experiment.** It is an unusually subsidized hackathon:

- Competitors donate weekend labor for a small probability of winning $10,000.
- The specification, incumbent reference product, motivated customer, and deadline are provided.
- Entrants are selected for enthusiasm and agent fluency.
- Nobody has yet borne years of security patches, support, integrations, onboarding, compliance, or customer turnover.

It demonstrates that an informed team can reproduce visible functionality extraordinarily cheaply. That is important. It does not demonstrate that the resulting system is a viable substitute over its lifecycle—or that customers will want to own it.

**BusyConf proves that code ownership is not the main cost, but it does not prove operations become a commodity.** It is a strong illustration of software’s total cost of ownership moving from creation to continuity. Yet a dead niche product on an aging Heroku stack cannot establish the margin structure of the whole market.

In fact, the evidence may support the opposite conclusion: operations become *more valuable* when code creation explodes. Hyperscalers, identity providers, observability vendors, security platforms, app stores, and managed databases collect substantial rents because they concentrate reliability and amortize complexity. Commodity inputs can still produce oligopoly economics when scale, switching costs, or control of a bottleneck matters.

**“Sessionboard’s $40,000 is scarcity rent; Sched’s $500 is honest ops cost” is asserted, not demonstrated.** Price is not cost plus a moral adjective. The $40,000 could include enterprise sales, implementation, support during a high-stakes live event, integrations, procurement friction, liability, and the customer’s willingness to pay to avoid reputational disaster. Conversely, $500 could be venture-subsidized, underpriced, narrowly scoped, or supported by a different customer mix. You need retention, gross-margin, support-cost, and willingness-to-switch evidence before calling one rent and the other honest.

**The thesis conflates value creation with value capture.** FDEs may create enormous value while capturing little of it. Professional services historically suffer from:

- Linear scaling with headcount
- Customer concentration and bargaining power
- Difficult quality control
- Bespoke maintenance obligations
- Low resale value and limited recurring leverage

The winners will not simply be “the people closest to the domain.” They will be actors who can convert repeated engagements into reusable assets: a platform, distribution channel, proprietary dataset, standard, ecosystem, or operational control point. The FDE is likely the *discovery and deployment interface* of the next software company, not necessarily its durable profit engine. Palantir-style economics work when services pull through a high-leverage platform; a consultancy producing 170 unrelated repositories is a much worse business.

**“Knowing what to build” is not safely scarce.** Gene’s advantage came from privileged archives and operator access, but agents helped convert those materials into usable requirements. As agents improve at interviewing users, mining tickets, observing workflows, analyzing telemetry, and generating prototypes, much of “domain understanding” becomes cheaper too.

The durable scarce resource may therefore shift from human understanding to:

- Permission to access private organizational context
- High-quality historical data and feedback
- Authority to change workflows
- Accountability when the system fails
- Trust sufficient to obtain all four

Proximity remains useful, but it is not intrinsically defensible. An incumbent with access to every customer interaction may learn faster than an excellent outsider.

**The thesis underweights the bespoke-software catastrophe.** Cheap generation can create millions of lightly understood forks. Every custom application becomes another authentication surface, dependency graph, data-retention regime, recovery plan, and abandoned repository. AI may reduce maintenance labor, but it also increases the volume and heterogeneity of things requiring maintenance.

Shared SaaS has a genuine economic advantage: one patch, migration, or learned edge case benefits the whole installed base. A bespoke clone forfeits that pooling. “Stewardship retainers” can therefore become SaaS again—except with worse standardization and margins.

**Jevons effects could enlarge SaaS rather than end it.** When software becomes cheaper, substantially more software gets attempted. The number of workflows worth automating expands, and previously uneconomic niches become markets. Total spending on software, compute, verification, integration, and operations may rise even while the cost per feature collapses. AI is more likely to destroy the existing unit of sale—the seat, feature bundle, or code license—than to reduce the total economic importance of software.

**“Software is the exhaust” is memorable and too strong.** Software remains reusable capital. A reliable codebase, protocol, integration network, or installed workflow can serve another customer at near-zero marginal cost. Human judgment may originate the product, but whatever learning gets successfully encoded and distributed becomes the scalable asset. Calling it exhaust risks missing the mechanism by which service work becomes compounding enterprise value.

## 2. Dynamics the thesis misses

**Distribution and customer acquisition become more—not less—decisive.** If 170 teams can build the product, building is no longer the filter. Reaching buyers, surviving procurement, earning a brand, getting embedded in an ecosystem, and becoming the default are the scarce complements. Cheap code may increase winner-take-most dynamics because supply explodes while buyer attention does not.

**Verification becomes a first-class industry.** Generating plausible software is cheap; establishing that it is secure, compliant, correct, recoverable, and fit for a consequential workflow is not. Tests, formal specifications, audit trails, evaluations, insurance, certification, and accountable operators become complements to codegen. In high-stakes domains, the economically valuable output may be evidence that the software can be trusted—not the software itself.

**The boundary of the firm changes.** “Build versus buy” becomes “own the workflow versus delegate the outcome.” Companies will internalize differentiating workflows while buying shared infrastructure and outsourcing undifferentiated outcomes. This produces a barbell: more internal/custom software on one side, and larger infrastructure/platform providers on the other. The vulnerable middle is generic application SaaS that neither owns a network nor delivers a guaranteed outcome.

A fourth, closely related dynamic is **the migration of pricing from access to outcomes**. When customers know a replacement can be generated cheaply, per-seat and feature-gated pricing becomes harder to defend. Vendors will try to charge for transactions, successful outcomes, risk transfer, uptime, or controlled access to valuable networks and data.

## 3. Answer to Gene’s kids

Economic value will accrue less to people who merely produce code and more to whoever owns the scarce complements around it: trusted access to a real problem, proprietary context and feedback, distribution, authority to change a workflow, responsibility for outcomes, and infrastructure that spreads reliability across many users. FDEs can be unusually powerful because they sit at the point where messy reality becomes working software, but services alone rarely capture the largest returns; the biggest fortunes will go to organizations that turn those engagements into a compounding platform, dataset, network, standard, or trusted operating capability. Code will be abundant, but reliable organizational change will remain scarce.

## Verdict

A sharp thesis about the collapse of code rents, weakened by treating a hackathon as a market, operations as a commodity, and FDE value creation as though it automatically produced durable value capture.
